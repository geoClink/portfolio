Planning the visit

  • Fly intoLondon Stansted Airport (STN)

    Stansted is the closest major airport to Cambridge, with direct trains to Cambridge station. Many international visitors also arrive via Heathrow or Gatwick and travel up through London.

  • Getting aroundWalking / Bike / Punt

    Central Cambridge is small and flat, so most people walk or cycle. Punting along the River Cam past the Backs is the classic way to see the colleges from the water.

  • Colors and teams

    Cambridge students speak of 'the Backs' (the college lawns along the river), 'supervisions' (small-group teaching) and 'the UL' for the University Library.

6 buildings to stamp

  1. 01

    King's College Chapel

    King's Parade, Cambridge CB2 1ST

    Built in phases from 1448 to 1515 by a succession of English kings, the chapel is one of the finest works of Perpendicular Gothic and has the world's largest fan vault. Its great windows of Flemish stained glass date from 1515 to 1531.

    Built
    1515
    Architect
    Reginald Ely, Simon Clerk and John Wastell
    Style
    Perpendicular Gothic
    Height
    29 m (95 ft)
    Today
    College chapel
  2. 02

    Wren Library

    Trinity College, Trinity Lane, Cambridge CB2 1TQ

    Christopher Wren raised Trinity College's library on an open colonnade above Nevile's Court, with tall windows to flood the reading room with light. Limewood carvings by Grinling Gibbons crown the bookcases, and the shelves hold treasures from Newton's own Principia to the manuscript of Winnie-the-Pooh.

    Built
    1695
    Architect
    Christopher Wren
    Style
    Classical
    Today
    College library
  3. 03

    Mathematical Bridge

    Queens' College, Silver Street, Cambridge CB3 9ET

    This wooden footbridge at Queens' College looks like a curved arch but is made entirely of straight timbers in a clever engineering design. It has been rebuilt twice, in 1866 and 1905, always to the same plan.

    Built
    1749
    Architect
    William Etheridge (design), James Essex (builder)
    Style
    Timber truss bridge
    Today
    College footbridge
  4. 04

    Bridge of Sighs

    St John's College, St John's Street, Cambridge CB2 1TP

    Henry Hutchinson's covered Gothic Revival bridge carries St John's College across the Cam between Third Court and New Court. Despite the name, it has little in common with its Venetian namesake beyond being covered and arched.

    Built
    1831
    Architect
    Henry Hutchinson
    Style
    Gothic Revival
    Today
    College bridge
  5. 05

    Senate House

    King's Parade, Cambridge CB2 1TN

    James Gibbs's Portland stone hall on King's Parade was meant as one side of a grand quadrangle that was never finished. It is where Cambridge students formally receive their degrees.

    Built
    1730
    Architect

    Style

  6. 06

    Cambridge University Library

    West Road, Cambridge CB3 9DR

    Giles Gilbert Scott, who also designed Bankside Power Station (now Tate Modern), gave the library a strong industrial character and a 157-foot tower. Opened in 1934, it holds around nine million items.

5 traditions to check off

Stamped! keeps these as a checklist, so you can tick each one off on the visit.

  • Nine Lessons and Carols at King's

    On Christmas Eve the King's College Choir sings the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols in King's College Chapel. Tickets come from a public ballot, with a standby queue for people who applied.

  • Corpus Clock

    The Corpus Clock, or Chronophage, is a gold-plated clock with no hands, set at street level on Corpus Christi College's Taylor Library at the corner of Bene't Street and Trumpington Street. Stop to watch its grasshopper-like figure as the clock runs.

  • Mathematical Bridge Myth

    A popular fable says Isaac Newton built Queens' College's Mathematical Bridge without nuts or bolts, and that nobody could put it back together after taking it apart. View the bridge from the river or Silver Street and judge the story yourself.

  • Newton's Apple Tree

    Outside Trinity College's Great Gate, below the rooms where Newton lived, grows an apple tree planted in 1954 and descended from the Woolsthorpe Manor tree linked to his story of gravity. Visitors stop on Trinity Street to see it.

  • Henry VIII's Chair Leg

    The statue of Henry VIII on Trinity's Great Gate is famous because students swapped his sceptre for a chair leg in the 19th century. Look up from Trinity Street to see what he is holding.

History

Founded 1209

From Wikipedia

The university's founding followed the arrival of scholars who left the University of Oxford for Cambridge after a dispute with local townspeople. The two ancient English universities, although sometimes described as rivals, share many common features and are often jointly referred to as Oxbridge.
