1,000 Year Capital

Kyoto architecture

Kyoto served as the imperial capital of Japan for over 1,000 years and remains the nation's cultural heart, with over 1,600 Buddhist temples and 400 Shinto shrines. It is the birthplace of the traditional tea ceremony and is world-renowned for its well-preserved Gion district, where geishas still walk the streets.

Before you go

  • Fly intoKansai Int'l (KIX)

    The airport is a marvel of civil engineering, built entirely on a massive man-made island in Osaka Bay to minimize noise pollution for the mainland. It was designed by architect Renzo Piano to resemble a giant airplane wing and is built on hydraulic jacks to compensate for the island's gradual settling into the seabed.

  • CurrencyJapanese Yen (¥)
  • LanguageJapanese

    The city is famous for 'Kyoto-ben,' a refined and soft-spoken dialect that reflects its imperial history. Standard Japanese is used by everyone, and English is widely available at major temples and shrines.

  • Getting aroundKyoto City Bus

    Because the city contains over 1,600 temples, the bus network is the primary way to reach most historic sites. Two subway lines and several private railways like Keihan and Hankyu connect Kyoto to Osaka and Nara. The Sagano Scenic Railway offers a nostalgic, open-air train ride through the beautiful Hozukyo Ravine.

12 landmarks in Kyoto

  1. 01

    Nijo Castle

    541 Nijojo-cho, Nakagyo Ward, Kyoto, 604-8301, Japan

    This historic fortress was built as the primary Kyoto residence for Tokugawa Ieyasu, the first Shogun of the Edo period. The Ninomaru Palace within the grounds is world-famous for its unique 'nightingale floors' designed to squeak and warn of assassins. Its interiors are decorated with opulent gold-leaf screens and intricate wood carvings created by the celebrated Kano school of artists. These grand designs were intentionally used to showcase the Shogun's immense wealth and power over the Imperial court. Today, the castle stands as a UNESCO World Heritage site and remains one of the best-preserved examples of feudal Japanese palace architecture.

  2. 02

    Fushimi Inari Taisha (Honden)

    68 Fukakusa Yabunouchicho, Fushimi Ward, Kyoto, 612-0882, Japan

    This site serves as the head shrine for Inari, the Shinto deity of rice, sake, and prosperity. While the shrine is famous for its thousands of bright vermilion torii gates, the main Honden building is a masterpiece of late 15th-century design. It features heavy ornamentation and a distinctive flowing roof style known as Nagare-zukuri. The shrine actually predates the relocation of the capital to Kyoto, marking it as one of the city's most ancient spiritual landmarks. Visitors often leave small fox statues as offerings, as foxes are considered the sacred messengers of the god Inari.

  3. 03

    Kyoto International Conference Center

    422 Iwakura Osagi-cho, Sakyo Ward, Kyoto, 606-0001, Japan

    This building is recognized as a premier landmark of Japanese Modernism and Brutalist architectural thought. The architect Sachio Otani used bold trapezoidal forms to reference traditional Japanese gables while creating a futuristic silhouette. It gained global fame as the site where the historic Kyoto Protocol was signed in 1997. The structure was specifically designed to sit in a perfect aesthetic dialogue with the surrounding Takara-ike pond and mountains. Its interior spaces are just as striking, featuring hexagonal motifs and grand concrete pillars that define the main halls.

  4. 04

    Sanjusangen-do

    657 Sanjusangendomawari-cho, Higashiyama Ward, Kyoto, 605-0941, Japan

    This incredibly long wooden hall is home to exactly one thousand and one life-sized statues of the Thousand-Armed Kannon. The name of the temple literally translates to 'Hall with thirty-three spaces between columns' to describe its elongated architecture. At 120 meters in length, it is currently the longest wooden structure in Japan. For centuries, the exterior veranda has been the site of Toshiya, a traditional archery competition of incredible skill. Each statue inside is carved from Japanese cypress and covered in gold leaf, creating a shimmering forest of deities.

  5. 05

    Heian Jingu Shrine

    Okazaki Nishitennocho, Sakyo Ward, Kyoto, 606-8341, Japan

    This shrine was constructed in 1895 to celebrate the 1100th anniversary of Kyoto's founding as the capital of Japan. The main buildings are designed as a partial two-thirds scale reproduction of the original Heian-period Imperial Palace. Its bright vermilion pillars and green-tiled roofs represent the classical architectural aesthetic of the year 794. The massive torii gate leading to the shrine is one of the largest in the country and dominates the Okazaki district. Behind the main buildings lies a vast stroll garden that is famous for its weeping cherry trees and scenic bridges.

  6. 06

    Apple Kyoto

    83 Tachiuri Nakano-cho, Shimogyo Ward, Kyoto, 600-8006, Japan

    This retail flagship is a masterclass in regional sensitivity, drawing heavy inspiration from traditional Japanese lanterns. The upper levels are wrapped in a translucent envelope made of a lightweight timber frame and special paper, referencing 'Machiya' architecture. A central atrium at the rear of the building connects the floors and allows light to penetrate the deep site. The ground floor features a transparent glass facade that seamlessly integrates the store into the busy Shijo Dori shopping street. Every material choice was made to respect Kyoto's history while providing a modern, communal space for technology.

  7. 07

    Kinkaku-ji (Golden Pavilion)

    1 Kinkakujicho, Kita Ward, Kyoto, 603-8361, Japan

    This Zen temple is world-famous because its top two floors are completely covered in shimmering gold leaf. The structure represents the extravagant Kitayama culture and sits perfectly balanced over a 'mirror pond' designed for reflection. Interestingly, each of its three floors represents a different historical style of Japanese architecture: Shinden, Samurai, and Zen. It was originally built as a retirement villa for the Shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu before being converted into a temple. The surrounding garden is a prime example of Muromachi-period design, intended to represent a Buddhist vision of paradise.

  8. 08

    Kyoto Station

    Higashishiokoji Kamadonocho, Shimogyo Ward, Kyoto, 600-8216, Japan

    The current station building provides a startling futuristic contrast to the city's ancient temples and shrines. This massive steel-and-glass structure features a fifteen-story exposed atrium known as the Matrix, which is open to the sky. It is one of Japan's largest buildings and functions as a self-contained city with a mall, hotel, and theater. An undulating roof and a high-altitude skyway tunnel offer unique architectural perspectives for travelers. The design was initially controversial due to its scale, but it has since become a beloved gateway for millions of international visitors.

  9. 09

    Kiyomizu-dera

    294 Kiyomizu, Higashiyama Ward, Kyoto, 605-0862, Japan

    This temple is iconic for its massive wooden stage that juts out over the hillside, offering incredible views of the city below. Remarkably, the entire main hall was constructed using traditional joinery techniques without the use of a single metal nail. The name of the temple translates to 'Pure Water Temple,' named after the Otowa Waterfall that flows within its grounds. It is a central part of the 'Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto' UNESCO World Heritage site. Visitors traditionally drink from the waterfall's three streams, which are said to grant longevity, success, and love.

  10. 10

    Kyoto City Kyocera Museum of Art

    124 Okazaki Enshojicho, Sakyo Ward, Kyoto, 606-8344, Japan

    As the oldest public art museum in Japan, this building originally opened its doors to the public in 1933. A recent renovation by Jun Aoki and Teppei Fujiwara successfully blended the original Imperial Crown style with a modern Glass Ribbon entrance. The addition of this transparent glass undulating facade allows the historic brickwork to remain visible while modernizing the visitor experience. It stands as a stunning example of how Kyoto integrates its architectural heritage with contemporary functional design. The museum's rooftop garden now provides a peaceful space for visitors to view the surrounding Okazaki cultural district.

  11. 11

    Katsura Imperial Villa

    Katsuramisono, Nishikyo Ward, Kyoto, 615-8014, Japan

    This villa is widely considered to be the absolute masterpiece of Japanese residential architecture and garden design. It perfected the Sukiya-zukuri style by blending formal courtly architecture with the rustic simplicity of a tea house. The minimalist lines and modular floor plans famously influenced 20th-century Western modernists like Walter Gropius and Le Corbusier. Every single room in the villa is meticulously designed to provide a perfectly framed view of the surrounding stroll garden. It remains under the control of the Imperial Household Agency, requiring special permission for public tours.

  12. 12

    Kyoto Tower

    721-1 Higashishiokojicho, Shimogyo Ward, Kyoto, 600-8216, Japan

    This tower is a rare and striking example of mid-century modernism located in a city primarily known for ancient temples. The structure was specifically designed to resemble a traditional Japanese candle standing on a pedestal. Unlike most towers of its era, it does not use a steel lattice frame but rather a series of welded steel cylinders. This lightweight monocoque structure stands atop a nine-story commercial building that houses a hotel and a public bath. It serves as a symbolic lighthouse for travelers, offering a panoramic 360-degree view of the entire Kyoto basin.
