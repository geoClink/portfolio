The Motor City

Detroit architecture

Detroit is the only major U.S. city where you must travel south to enter Canada, crossing into Windsor, Ontario. Known as the 'Motor City,' it revolutionized the modern world through the automotive industry and the 'Motown Sound' that defined a generation of American music.

Before you go

  • Fly intoDetroit Metro (DTW)

    The McNamara Terminal features a world-famous 700-foot underground tunnel that uses synchronized LED lights and an original soundscape to entertain travelers. It is a major international gateway for Delta Air Lines, frequently serving as a primary hub for flights to Asia and Europe.

  • CurrencyUS Dollar ($)
  • LanguageEnglish

    The local dialect is sometimes called the 'Michigan Mush,' noted for its nasal vowels and the use of glottal stops that make 't' sounds in the middle of words disappear. It is a friendly, gritty, and soulful way of speaking that mirrors the city's resilient character.

  • Getting aroundPeople Mover & QLine

    The People Mover is an automated rail system that moves in a continuous loop around the downtown core. The QLine streetcar services the historic Woodward Avenue corridor with modern, battery-capable vehicles. Regional SMART buses connect the city center to the surrounding tri-county suburban areas.

    First National Building

    660 Woodward Ave, Detroit, MI 48226

    This neoclassical skyscraper was designed with a unique 'U' shape to provide every office with access to natural light. Its grand facade is finished in Bedford limestone and is famously adorned with massive Corinthian columns. Originally serving as the headquarters for the First National Bank, it remains a vital anchor of the Woodward Avenue streetscape. The building stands as a testament to the early 20th-century economic boom that transformed the city's downtown core. Today, it serves as a bustling mixed-use hub housing high-profile offices and popular ground-floor dining options.

    The Z Garage

    1234 Library St, Detroit, MI 48226

    The Z is a zig-zagging parking structure that famously doubles as a world-class public art gallery. Its distinctive shape was a creative architectural solution designed to maximize the utility of a tight corner lot. The structure is most famous for 'The Belt,' a public alleyway that features massive murals by international street artists. This project proves that even functional urban infrastructure can be elevated through high-end artistic design. It has become a central social landmark in Detroit, bridging the gap between utilitarian parking and cultural destination.

  4. 04

    Packard Automotive Plant

    1580 E Grand Blvd, Detroit, MI 48211

    This facility represented the first industrial application of reinforced concrete, a method later known as the 'Kahn System.' The innovative structural design allowed for massive open floor plans and expansive windows that improved factory safety. While it eventually became the world's most famous industrial ruin, its structural skeleton remains a symbol of Detroit's engineering prowess. Much of the site was demolished in 2024, yet parts remain as a historic testament to the city's automotive legacy. The remaining structures are currently slated for future redevelopment to preserve their important role in American industrial history.

    One Kennedy Square

    777 Woodward Ave, Detroit, MI 48226

    This building stands as a primary symbol of Detroit's downtown revitalization efforts during the early 2000s. It is easily recognized by its vibrant emerald-green glass curtain wall that faces Campus Martius Park. A unique engineering feat, the entire 13-story office building sits directly on top of a 10-story underground parking garage. The design was intended to provide a modern and transparent contrast to the heavy stone skyscrapers surrounding it. It serves as a central hub for corporate offices and offers prime views of the city's most active public square.

    McGregor Memorial Conference Center

    495 West Ferry Mall, Detroit, MI 48202

    Designed by Minoru Yamasaki, this building is a world-renowned masterpiece of the 'New Formalism' architectural style. The structure features a stunning skylit garden court and is surrounded by a peaceful reflective pool. Its white quartz and marble facade utilizes repeating geometric shapes to create a sense of profound serenity. It is widely considered one of the most beautiful examples of mid-century modern design in the United States. Located on the Wayne State campus, it continues to serve as an elegant venue for conferences and university events.

    Renaissance Center

    400 Renaissance Dr, Detroit, MI 48243

    Often described as a 'city within a city,' this complex consists of seven interconnected skyscrapers along the riverfront. The central tower is the tallest building in Michigan and houses the world's tallest all-hotel skyscraper. Originally designed to revitalize the waterfront, it serves as the global headquarters for General Motors. Massive renovations in the early 2000s removed concrete barriers to make the complex more integrated with the city streets. It remains the most iconic silhouette in the Detroit skyline and a hub for international business and tourism.

    Michigan Central

    2001 15th St, Detroit, MI 48216

    Once the tallest rail station in the world, this Beaux-Arts landmark sat abandoned for three decades before its recent revival. The Ford Motor Company acquired and meticulously restored the structure to serve as a modern technology hub. Its grand waiting room features soaring Guastavino tile arches and massive, majestic limestone columns. The building now serves as the primary anchor for a 30-acre mobility and innovation campus. This restoration is widely regarded as a pivotal turning point for the historic Corktown neighborhood.

    Often referred to as Detroit's Largest Art Object, the Fisher Building was funded by the wealthy Fisher brothers. The stunning interior arcade is lined with forty different types of marble and exquisite gold leaf detailing. Its grand gilded roof was originally covered in real gold leaf before being altered during World War II. Designers opted for green terra cotta during the war to avoid the building becoming a target for pilots. This Art Deco jewel remains one of the most prestigious office and theater addresses in the city.

It holds the historic distinction of being the first theater designed with a built-in speaker system for talkies. Today, it remains the crown jewel of Detroit's entertainment district and hosts world-class performances.

    General Motors Building (Cadillac Place)

    3044 W Grand Blvd, Detroit, MI 48202

    Designed by Albert Kahn to be the largest office building in the world at the time, this massive Neoclassical structure is legendary. It served as the global headquarters for General Motors for seventy-five years before the company moved to the Renaissance Center. The design consists of four parallel fifteen-story wings connected by a central spine to ensure every office has a window. Its limestone exterior and Corinthian colonnade symbolize the immense power of the American auto industry during the 1920s. Today, the building has been renamed Cadillac Place and primarily houses various State of Michigan government offices.

    Lafayette Towers

    1301 Orleans St, Detroit, MI 48207

    Designed by the legendary Mies van der Rohe, these twin residential towers are a landmark of the International Style. Built as part of an ambitious urban renewal project, they feature sleek curtain wall glass facades and aluminum frames. The towers sit within a beautifully landscaped park to embody the famous Tower in a Park urban philosophy. This minimalist approach to residential living influenced city planning and modern architecture on a global scale. They remain a highly desirable residential address for those seeking mid-century modern aesthetics in downtown Detroit.

    Buhl Building

    535 Griswold St, Detroit, MI 48226

    The Buhl Building is renowned for its unique cruciform 'cross' plan view which optimizes natural light for every office. This architectural choice was a clever engineering solution for high-rises in the era before modern air conditioning. The facade is a masterful blend of Neo-Gothic and Romanesque styles featuring intricate terra cotta craftsmanship. Visitors are often drawn to the historic 'Buhl Bar' located within the lobby's ornate interior. It stands as one of the most sophisticated examples of the early skyscraper boom in Detroit's financial district.

The building was originally constructed for the Vinton Company and has been meticulously preserved over the decades. It has recently found new life as luxury condominiums while maintaining its early 20th-century commercial charm.

    Cadillac Tower

    65 Cadillac Square, Detroit, MI 48226

    This 40-story Neo-Gothic skyscraper was once the tallest building in the world outside of New York and Chicago. It remains a prominent landmark in the Detroit skyline thanks to its tiered setbacks and decorative peak. For many years, the building's side was famous for hosting a massive mural of Detroit Red Wings legend Steve Yzerman. Its 'high-waisted' design is typical of early skyscrapers that sought to maximize height while adhering to light and air regulations. Today, the tower serves as a hub for various government offices and retail spaces in the heart of the city.

    The hotel was built upon the historic site of Fort Shelby and was designed to accommodate Detroit’s thriving rail travelers. After surviving a long period of closure and neglect, the property underwent a total renovation to become a modern Hilton suites hotel.     Motown Museum (Hitsville U.S.A.)

    2648 W Grand Blvd, Detroit, MI 48208

    This humble residential structure serves as the birthplace of the globally recognized Motown Sound. Founded by Berry Gordy Jr. in 1959, the former photography studio was quickly converted into a legendary hit-making machine. The building's iconic 'Hitsville U.S.A.' sign still hangs proudly over the front porch of the world-famous Studio A. Inside these walls, music legends like Stevie Wonder and The Supremes recorded the tracks that defined a generation. It remains a premier global pilgrimage site for music lovers and is preserved exactly as it appeared during the 1960s.

    Wright-Kay Building

    1500 Woodward Ave, Detroit, MI 48226

    Originally known as the Schwankovsky Temple of Music, this structure remains one of Detroit’s most picturesque Victorian landmarks. It is easily identified by its massive corner turret and rich, dark red brickwork that exemplifies the Queen Anne style. During its early years, musicians would perform from the upper floors so their melodies would drift down to shoppers on Woodward Avenue. The building's high-pitched roof and ornate stonework reflect the architectural sophistication of Detroit's late 19th-century boom. Today, it houses the award-winning restaurant Wright & Co. on its second floor, continuing its legacy as a center of city life.
