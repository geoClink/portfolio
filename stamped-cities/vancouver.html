Rain City

Vancouver architecture

Vancouver has the highest population density in North America after New York City, yet it is surrounded by some of the most pristine wilderness on the continent. It is a city where you can theoretically ski on a mountain and go sailing in the ocean all in the same afternoon.

Before you go

  • Fly intoVancouver Int'l (YVR)

    YVR has been voted the best airport in North America for 12 consecutive years, known for its massive indoor aquariums and extensive collection of Indigenous Totem poles. It is a major trans-Pacific hub, serving as the primary connection point between Canada and the major markets of Asia.

  • CurrencyCanadian Dollar ($)
  • LanguageEnglish

    While English is the official language, Vancouver’s status as a Pacific gateway means that Cantonese, Mandarin, and Punjabi are also widely spoken. This multicultural blend creates a linguistic environment where many residents are comfortably multilingual.

  • Getting aroundSkyTrain

    The SkyTrain is the longest fully automated driverless rapid transit system in the world. The SeaBus is a unique passenger-only ferry that crosses the Burrard Inlet every 15 minutes. TransLink also operates the West Coast Express, a commuter rail service for the eastern Fraser Valley.

15 landmarks in Vancouver

  1. 01

    Marine Building

    355 Burrard St, Vancouver, BC V6C 2G8

    Once the tallest building in the British Empire, this Art Deco masterpiece is famous for its intricate 'marine-themed' ornamentation. The entrance is a riot of terracotta sea creatures, ships, and explorers. The lobby, designed to look like a Mayan temple under the sea, features clock faces with sea creatures instead of numbers and a blue-tinted ceiling that mimics the ocean depths. Massive brass doors and elaborate stone carvings further emphasize the building's prestige and historical significance. It remains one of the most beloved examples of 1930s architecture in all of North America.

  2. 02

    Vancouver Art Gallery (Old Courthouse)

    750 Hornby St, Vancouver, BC V6Z 2H7

    Originally the city's provincial courthouse, this Neoclassical landmark is constructed from granite and features massive Ionic columns. Two iconic stone lions guard the Georgia Street entrance, serving as popular meeting spots for locals. It was converted into an art gallery in 1983 by Arthur Erickson, who added the famous 'staircase' galleries and rooftop garden while preserving the grand rotunda. The interior spaces now house a vast collection of works by Emily Carr and other significant Canadian artists. The building serves as the civic heart of Vancouver, often hosting major public protests, rallies, and celebrations.

  3. 03

    Canada Place

    999 Canada Place, Vancouver, BC V6C 3E2

    The crown jewel of Vancouver's waterfront was designed to look like a giant sailing ship. Its five distinctive white Teflon-coated fiberglass sails have become the primary icon of the city's skyline. Built for Expo 86, it serves as a cruise ship terminal, convention center, and luxury hotel. It is a prime example of 'Literal Expressionism' meant to evoke the city's rich maritime heritage. Visitors can walk the Promenade to enjoy panoramic views of the Burrard Inlet and the North Shore mountains.

  4. 04

    Vancouver Public Library (Central Branch)

    350 W Georgia St, Vancouver, BC V6B 6B4

    Inspired by the Roman Colosseum, this massive structure occupies an entire city block in the heart of downtown. It features a circular, detached wall containing reading galleries that wrap around the main rectangular library building. The design creates a soaring internal 'urban room' or atrium that acts as a bustling public piazza. It is one of the most recognizable examples of Postmodernism in Canada and a frequent filming location for Hollywood productions. The upper floors also include a lush public rooftop garden that offers a quiet escape from the city noise.

  5. 05

    Hotel Vancouver

    900 W Georgia St, Vancouver, BC V6C 2W6

    This is the third hotel of its name and stands as a classic 'Railway Gothic' landmark. Its steeply pitched copper roof—now oxidized to a brilliant green—is adorned with grotesque carvings and mythical gargoyles. It was built by the CN and CP Railways and was long the tallest building in the city. It remains a symbol of old-world Canadian luxury amidst a modern sea of glass skyscrapers. Many visitors come specifically to see its grand ballroom and experience its storied, high-society atmosphere.

  6. 06

    Museum of Anthropology (MOA)

    6393 NW Marine Dr, Vancouver, BC V6T 1Z2

    A masterpiece of 'West Coast Modernism' perched on a scenic cliff at the University of British Columbia. Arthur Erickson modeled the concrete post-and-beam structure after the traditional cedar houses of the Haida people. The Great Hall features 15-meter-high glass walls that offer stunning views of the mountains and sea. This design seamlessly integrates a world-renowned First Nations totem pole collection with the surrounding natural landscape. It is widely considered one of the most important architectural achievements in Canadian history.

  7. 07

    The Electra (B.C. Electric Building)

    970 Burrard St, Vancouver, BC V6Z 2R4

    The Electra was Vancouver's first major modern skyscraper and a bold departure from traditional design. Its floor plan is shaped like an elongated diamond, meant to ensure every desk was near a window for natural light. The exterior features colorful tile mosaics by artist B.C. Binning that add a touch of mid-century flair. Formerly the headquarters of the electric utility, it was converted into residential condos in the 1990s. This conversion pioneered the city's residential-tower movement that defines the downtown core today.

  8. 08

    Bloedel Conservatory

    4600 Cambie St, Vancouver, BC V6G 1Z4

    Sitting at the highest point in the city within Queen Elizabeth Park, this triodetic dome is made of 1,490 acrylic bubbles. It was a gift to the city to celebrate the Canadian Centennial and promote botanical education. Inside, it houses a lush tropical ecosystem with hundreds of free-flying exotic birds and rare plants. Its futuristic, 'space-age' appearance remains a striking contrast to the surrounding manicured gardens. The facility provides a climate-controlled escape that feels like a tropical vacation regardless of the Vancouver weather.

  9. 09

    Living Shangri-La

    1128 W Georgia St, Vancouver, BC V6E 0A8

    Living Shangri-La is currently the tallest building in Vancouver, dominating the downtown skyline. This slender glass spire is a textbook example of 'Vancouverism'—the urban planning trend of thin towers on podiums. The building changes its shape slightly as it rises to maximize views and structural stability. It features a curated public art garden at its base that welcomes pedestrians into its luxury environment. Within its walls, it houses a world-class hotel and some of the city's most expensive residential penthouses.

  10. 10

    Sun Tower

    128 W Pender St, Vancouver, BC V6B 1R8

    Famous for its green faux-copper dome and the 'nine maidens'—nine terracotta caryatids that support the cornice. When first built, it held the title of the tallest building in the British Empire. It originally housed the Vancouver World newspaper and was a hub for local journalism. The building features a highly decorative Beaux-Arts base that contrasts with the utilitarian office floors above. Its distinctive silhouette remains one of the most recognizable features of the historic Gastown and Chinatown border.

  11. 11

    Vancouver House

    1480 Howe St, Vancouver, BC V6Z 0G5

    A feat of structural engineering, this tower starts as a narrow triangle at the base to avoid a nearby bridge. It gradually 'twists' and expands into a large rectangle at the top of the structure. This creates a dramatic cantilever that seems to defy gravity when viewed from the street level. It has won numerous global awards for its 'Neo-Futurist' design and innovative use of space. The building has quickly become one of the most photographed modern landmarks in all of Canada.

  12. 12

    Gastown Steam Clock

    305 Water St, Vancouver, BC V6B 1A5

    While technically a small structure, this is arguably Vancouver's most famous and photographed landmark. It was originally built to cover a steam grate and utilize the city's underground heating system. The clock uses a series of steam whistles to chime the Westminster Quarters every fifteen minutes. Its Victorian-style design fits perfectly into the cobblestone aesthetic of Vancouver's oldest historic neighborhood. Tourists gather around it hourly to watch the steam billow out from its copper roof.

  13. 13

    Harbour Centre

    555 W Hastings St, Vancouver, BC V6B 4N6

    Known for the 'Vancouver Lookout,' a UFO-shaped observation deck perched atop a brutalist office tower. The building is famous for its exterior glass elevators that offer a thrilling ride to the top. It was once the tallest building in the city and remains a primary landmark for navigation. The revolving restaurant at the peak provides a 360-degree view of the entire Lower Mainland. It continues to be a top destination for tourists wanting to see the city from a bird's-eye perspective.

  14. 14

    Woodward’s Building

    101 W Hastings St, Vancouver, BC V6B 1H4

    Once the city's premier department store, the site was the focus of intense community protests after the company went bankrupt. The modern redevelopment saved the original 1903 facade and the iconic 'W' neon sign on the roof. It is now a mixed-use complex that blends social housing with luxury condos and university space. The project serves as a complex symbol of the gentrification within the Downtown Eastside neighborhood. Its central atrium features a massive photographic mural depicting the historic Gastown riots.

  15. 15

    The Qube

    1333 W Georgia St, Vancouver, BC V6E 4V3

    The Qube is one of Vancouver's most unusual buildings due to its top-down construction method. The central concrete core was completed first, and then the floors were suspended from the top using steel cables. This engineering choice results in a massive glass cube that appears to float effortlessly above the ground. It survived a 1960s plan to demolish it and was eventually converted into high-end residential lofts. The building is often cited as a prime example of structuralism and remains a unique part of the skyline.
