Why I Built It

At the Apple Developer Academy, I was on a team of five building for a real client. It was a great place to learn how real client work runs, and it showed me a problem I hadn't thought much about: we logged our hours by hand. That manual process cost real time, and about a month of hours were lost before Tally was far enough along to use.

We needed one place where all five of us could log hours, stay in sync, and show the client what we'd actually worked on. Every tool we tried was either built for solo freelancers with no team layer, or bloated enterprise software that cost more than the project was worth. Tally is the tool we needed: it works for one person or a whole team, runs on the devices people already use, and doesn't charge a subscription just to log hours.

Those lost hours also shaped where Tally went next. The fix wasn't a better form for typing hours in. It was meeting people in the tools they already use, so a timer starts from the issue you're working on and the time lands back where your team will see it.

What's New

Tally is on version 1.8. Beyond the timer, here's what it does today.

Jira, Linear, Notion & GitHub

Start a timer from a Jira ticket, Linear issue or Notion task. When you save, the time is posted back to it. GitHub finds commits you didn't track and offers to add the session.

Calendar

Client meetings from Google Calendar, or a link from Proton, Outlook or iCloud, show up as sessions to add, and a notification can nudge you when one starts or ends untimed.

Contracts

Weekly or monthly minimums, makeup hours that carry short weeks forward, time off that doesn't count as a shortfall, and a week-by-week view of where each contract stands.

Teams

Team contracts, plus admins who can add or edit a member's time. Those sessions carry an "Added by" tag, and the member gets a notification.

Invoicing & reports

Send Stripe invoices straight from Tally, share a private client report link, and get a weekly recap of your hours, what they're worth, and the time that looks untracked.

Claude connector

A Claude connector (MCP) lets you ask about your hours in plain language: what you worked on this week, how a contract is tracking, or what you've invoiced.

See It Work

Short clips from the web app, recorded at phone width. Pick one, or let them play in turn.

Architecture

Platforms. Native SwiftUI apps for iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch, and Mac (a true macOS target, not Catalyst), a React web app, and a Capacitor Android app, all on one Supabase backend. A visionOS version is in development.

Payments. StoreKit, Google Play Billing, and Stripe all feed one Supabase subscriptions table — no third-party billing service. Writes are server-only — Stripe through a webhook, App Store purchases verified against Apple's signature by an Edge Function — so a client can't grant itself Pro. Universal Purchase means one App Store payment covers iPhone and Mac.

Push notifications. A Supabase Edge Function delivers through APNs and Firebase Cloud Messaging — signing an ES256 JWT per request, routing sandbox vs. production, and pruning dead tokens. Permission is requested after the first tracked session rather than at launch, and a device's token is removed on sign-out.

Automated email. A weekly summary email of each user's hours, and a re-engagement email for users who've gone quiet. Scheduled jobs in Supabase call Edge Functions that send through Resend, with signed one-click unsubscribe links.

Integrations. Jira and Linear connect with a personal API token, which is encrypted before it's stored. When a session is saved, an Edge Function posts it back as a Jira worklog or a Linear comment. GitHub is read-only and limited to the repositories you choose. The Claude connector is an MCP server that answers questions from the same data the apps use.

How It Works

Free to start, Pro for $9.99 one-time (unlimited clients and full history), or Business for $4.99 per user per month (teams, invoicing, and Stripe payments).

  • Timer with pause/resume, or log hours by duration or start–end time
  • Weekly goals per client, budgets that turn red when exceeded, and streaks
  • Reports, an activity calendar, and CSV export with times, team, rate, and amount
  • Invoices from billable hours, paid through Stripe Connect straight into the freelancer's own account
  • Team workspaces with owner/admin/member roles enforced by row-level security, not just the UI
  • Sign in with email, Apple, or Google; voice control and keyboard shortcuts on the web

Native macOS App

A fully native macOS target — not Catalyst — with Mac patterns throughout: sidebar navigation, right-click to edit or delete sessions, and Mac-specific sign-in (Apple and email; Google has no Mac SDK).

Mac and iOS share the same SwiftUI source, with iOS-only APIs excluded by #if os(iOS) guards. A shared bundle ID enables Universal Purchase: buy on iPhone, unlocked on Mac.

Engineering Highlights

  • Shared business logic: contract math (minimums, makeup hours, time off, caps) lives in one plain JavaScript module used by both the web app and the server functions, and is mirrored in Swift for the native apps, with tests that keep the two in step
  • Automated tests: Deno tests for the server functions, Swift tests for the Mac app, and end-to-end browser tests that sign in, run a timer, and save a session against a local stack
  • Security: row-level security on every table, integration tokens encrypted at rest, signed one-click unsubscribe links, and client report links that use unguessable tokens and can be turned off at any time
  • CI: GitHub Actions workflows build the iPhone, Mac, and visionOS apps and lint and build the web app
  • Local backend: a full Supabase stack runs in Docker with seeded test accounts, so testing never touches production
  • Versioned database: schema changes ship as migrations, tested locally before they reach production
  • Releases: every App Store version is tagged with notes on GitHub, and Dependabot keeps dependencies current
  • Privacy: analytics load only after consent, and every marketing email has one-click unsubscribe

What Was Hard

My first cross-platform project: iOS, watchOS, Mac, web, and Android all reading and writing one backend, so every layer has to be right at once.

Payments were the hardest part: three stores feeding one subscription record, written only by the server so nobody can grant themselves Pro. Getting through App Review took nearly 10 rejections, each for a different guideline, before it shipped.

The native Mac target shares iOS source, so iOS-only APIs had to be guarded across dozens of views, and Mac needed its own sign-in flow because Google Sign-In has no Mac SDK.

User Research

After launch, I manually reviewed the email addresses of users who had created accounts and reached out to 30 of them directly. Most didn't respond — but one did: a senior product designer who has become an ongoing collaborator and the clearest signal I have of who Tally is really for.

Her feedback drove concrete product decisions:

  • Billing cycles per client — she bills different clients on different schedules. I added monthly (by day of month) and weekly (by weekday) billing cycles, plus a "Last Billing Period" button on invoices that auto-fills the correct date range.
  • Client settings in the Account tab — she wanted rates and billing cycles accessible without leaving the main app flow. I moved Client Settings into the Account tab with TipKit contextual tips on first use.
  • Invoice persistence — name, email, and tax rate now persist across invoices so she isn't re-entering the same info every time.
  • Billing cycle reminder notifications — a daily push fires at the start of each billing period ("Time to invoice Acme Corp.") so she never misses an invoice window.

One respondent who explains their workflow is worth more than a hundred survey answers.

The Outcome

Nearly 300 downloads and 63K+ App Store impressions since launch, built and shipped solo. Tally is on version 1.8 on the App Store for iPhone (Mac 1.8 is in review) and in closed testing on Google Play, with one account across all of them.
